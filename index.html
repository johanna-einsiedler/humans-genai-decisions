Humans & GenAI in Decision Tasks

Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone (2024) pooled three years of experiments and found that human–AI teams, on average, did worse than the better of the human or the AI working alone. Those experiments end in mid-2023, before large language models became everyday tools. This page keeps their evidence on decision tasks and adds new experiments with generative AI.

In the original units

The three figures below use the scores as the papers report them, for outcomes on a proportion scale (accuracy and similar). They need no standard deviations, so more of the GenAI studies can take part.

Who is stronger alone, and does teaming up help?

Each point is one comparison: the human-alone score against the AI-alone score. Above the diagonal the AI is stronger alone. The colour says whether the combination beat the stronger of the two.

Who is most often the worst of the three?

For every comparison: which of human alone, AI alone and the team scored lowest. All decision-task comparisons count here, whatever their scale.

How much is gained or lost against the stronger one?

The combination’s score minus the best solo score, in percentage points, split by who is stronger alone. Zero means the team did exactly as well as its stronger member.

How big must the AI’s lead be before the AI alone wins?

Moving right, the AI’s advantage over the human grows. The line is the share of comparisons, among all those with an AI advantage up to that point, in which the AI alone beat the human–AI combination.

The AI’s lead, bin by bin

Only comparisons where the AI alone is stronger, grouped by the size of its lead: how often does the team still beat the AI alone? In the experiments of it often does when the lead is small, and rarely once the lead passes ten to fifteen points.
